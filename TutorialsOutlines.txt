Lab 1 — Clone a Classical PUF (25 min)

Goal: Let attendees experience first-hand why classical PUFs can fall short before introducing any quantum concepts. The key insight is that 
the responses are classical bits: they can be copied, stored and, importantly, learned. Therefore, unclonable hardware does not necessarily 
imply an unclonable function. The quantum approaches introduced later in the tutorial can then be assessed against this failure.

Activity: Working in pairs using a shared notebook, attendees will:

    Simulate a population of 64-stage arbiter PUFs using the additive delay model.

    Compute standard metrics themselves — uniqueness ≈ 0.50, reliability ≈ 99%, and uniformity ≈ 0.50 — and 
    inspect the intra-/inter-device distributions.

    Mount a modelling attack by training logistic regression on observed CRPs and watching the clone accuracy 
    increase from approximately 71% with 50 CRPs to approximately 95% with 500 CRPs, without ever accessing the physical PUF.

Suggested timing: 3 min setup + 15 min guided work + 5 min plenary discussion (“Which assumption did we actually break?”) + 2 min buffer.

Reference: PyPUF documentation.
Lab 2 — Clone a QR-PUF (25 min)

Goal: Demonstrate experimentally how adding quantum-encoded responses to the Lab 1 
      arbiter PUF changes the cloning problem. Following the hybrid-PUF construction 
      in the Quantum Lock paper (arXiv:2110.09469), the lab explores how modelling attacks 
      require substantially more CRPs when the attacker has access only to quantum-encoded 
      responses, while the basis information encoded using BB84 cannot be directly learned 
      from a single copy.

Activity: Attendees will encode each pair of arbiter-PUF response bits into one BB84 qubit, 
          with one bit determining the basis and the other the encoded value. They will then:

    Explore Eve’s information limits numerically, including why the basis bit cannot be 
    distinguished from a single copy and the limits on recovering the value bit.

    Replay the Lab 1 ML attack using the resulting noisy labels and compare the 
    number of CRPs required to reach a given modelling accuracy.

    Examine how the probability of successful forgery decreases as 
    the number of verified qubits increases.

    Discuss challenge reuse and the lockdown countermeasure proposed in the paper.
